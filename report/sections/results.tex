\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline-results}
% Verified run summary and artifact provenance: BASELINE_CLASS_RESULTS.md.
We checked 100 validation messages and 200 test messages independently,
starting each with an empty registry. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-detection}, precision exceeded 90\% on both splits.
On the test split, Qwen detected 39 of the 83 labeled positive messages
and missed 44. The validation split had 20 true positives, one false
positive, eight false negatives, and 71 true negatives. The test split also had three false positives and 114 true negatives.

We also replayed six preselected WildChat prefixes of 16 user turns each.
Qwen retained the injected rule in all six final registries. Given the final
registry and a follow-up question, it followed the rule in four of six
replies (66.67\%). In the two misses, Qwen skipped restating the question and
included imperial conversions despite a metric-only rule, respectively.

in approximately 23 minutes and 19 seconds, with zero recorded inference

tokens for extraction, plus 414 prompt tokens and 455 generated tokens for
follow-up replies.

\subsection{Jev gate}
\label{sec:jev-results}
% Owner: Jalil Jabbarli.
We ran the gate in four steps, each answering a question raised by the one
before. Every turn is scored once and thresholds are applied to the saved
scores. Table~\ref{tab:jev} summarizes the runs. Variant A is the two-question
gate of the Method section; variant B is introduced below.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Jev gate runs. $\tau$ is the highest threshold with validation
  recall $\geq 0.95$, fixed before scoring test. Saved $=1-K/N$.}
  \label{tab:jev}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrrrr}
    \toprule
    Run & Gate & $\tau$ & Recall & FP & Saved & AUROC \\
    \midrule
    Pilot (val, 10)  & A & 0.05 & 1.00 & 3  & 20\% & 0.92 \\
    Val (100)        & A & 0.05 & 1.00 & 33 & 39\% & 0.97 \\
    Val (100)        & B & 0.80 & 1.00 & 1  & 71\% & 0.997 \\
    Test (200)       & A & 0.05 & 0.98 & 67 & 26\% & 0.95 \\
    Test (200)       & B & 0.80 & 0.92 & 15 & 54\% & 0.95 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{1. Pilot (10 validation turns)} A balanced sample (5 positive,
5 negative) to check that the gate runs end to end and to look for early
failure modes. Injected constraints scored 0.86--0.99 and all negatives
$\leq 0.11$, but both SWE-Natural positives scored below 0.10.

\paragraph{2. Full validation, variant A (100 turns)} To pick $\tau$ on the
real class mix. Injected constraints averaged 0.96 and random turns 0.05,
but SWE-Natural positives averaged only 0.14. Reaching 0.95 recall required
$\tau=0.05$, which saves 39\% of extractor calls.

\paragraph{3. Error analysis and variant B} The missed SWE-Natural
constraints are task-level remarks inside GitHub issues (e.g., ``I do not
want to do it in the common definitions!''), not ``from now on'' rules. The
paper defines a side constraint broadly, as a requirement on how a task is
carried out that persists through the session, and its code uses a separate,
broader extractor prompt for SWE-Natural~\cite{compactionintegrity}. Our two
questions, like our baseline extractor, follow the stricter general prompt.
Variant B adds a third question, whether the message restricts \emph{how}
the requested work is done (approaches to use or avoid, things to preserve,
approvals), and takes the maximum of all three. Our first wording paraphrased
two validation turns, so we rewrote it with neutral examples absent from both
splits; the scores were nearly identical. On validation, B separated the
classes almost perfectly (Table~\ref{tab:jev}).

\paragraph{4. Test (200 turns, scored once)} To check whether the validation
gain holds with $\tau$ fixed. It held only partly. At the locked $\tau=0.80$,
B reached recall 0.92, below the 0.95 target. Five of its seven misses are
test-only injected constraints without ``for the rest of this session''
(e.g., ``reply in bullet points only''), which A also scores low. B also
flags detailed one-off WildChat requests (e.g., ``You are an experienced art
educator\ldots''). At matched recall, B still saves more calls than A (34\%
vs.\ 26\% at recall $\geq 0.98$; 54\% vs.\ 46\% at 0.92), and AUROC is
similar (0.954 vs.\ 0.949). The gate costs about \$0.00004 per turn
(\$0.00005 for B) with 0.17\,s mean latency.

\paragraph{Conclusion} Jev ranks constraint turns well (test AUROC 0.95),
but a threshold tuned on validation did not transfer to test. Most errors
come from two sources: what counts as a constraint (task-level restrictions
vs.\ lasting rules), and constraints stated without explicit scope words.
Asking about task-level restrictions fixes the first for SWE-Natural but adds
false positives on detailed WildChat requests, so the net gain is modest.

\paragraph{Next steps} (i) Measure gate recall against the constraints the
baseline extractor actually returns, since our goal is to match the
baseline, not the labels. (ii) Ask the third question only for agent or
coding tasks. (iii) Report calls saved, cost, and latency on the stream set.
(iv) Compare Jev with other cheap decision models at equal recall, such as a
small open LLM prompted with the same yes/no questions and a small encoder
classifier fine-tuned on our labeled turns, to show whether gains come from
Jev itself or from gating in general.
